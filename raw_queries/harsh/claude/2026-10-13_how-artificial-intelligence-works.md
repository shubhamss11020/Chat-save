# How artificial intelligence works

## User (2026-10-13 07:24:42 EDT)

Explain how artificial intelligence works in simple terms.

## Claude (2026-10-13 07:24:56 EDT)

> *Thinking:*
>

Artificial intelligence is software that learns patterns from examples instead of following rules a person wrote out by hand.

**The basic idea**

Suppose you want a program that recognizes cats in photos. Writing rules for it ("pointy ears, whiskers, fur...") fails quickly, because cats come in endless shapes, poses, and lighting. So instead you show the program thousands of photos labeled "cat" or "not cat" and let it work out for itself what separates them. That's machine learning, and it's the core of most modern AI.

**How the learning works**

1. **Start with a flexible model.** Most modern AI uses a *neural network*, a large web of simple math units loosely inspired by the brain. At first its internal settings (millions or billions of adjustable numbers called *parameters*) are random, so its guesses are garbage.
2. **Make a guess and measure the error.** The model looks at an example and predicts an answer. The program compares that to the correct answer and calculates how wrong it was.
3. **Nudge the settings.** The program adjusts the parameters slightly so the same mistake becomes a bit less likely.
4. **Repeat a huge number of times.** After millions of examples, the model gets good at the task, and it can handle new examples it has never seen.

**How chatbots like me fit in**

Language models are trained on enormous amounts of text with one simple goal: predict the next word. To predict well, the model has to pick up grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, and writing styles. Later, people refine it by giving feedback on which responses are helpful, so it becomes a useful assistant rather than just a text-completer.

**What AI is and isn't**

- It's very good at spotting patterns in data, which is why it can translate, summarize, recognize images, and write code.
- It doesn't "understand" the way people do, and it can be confidently wrong, because it's generating likely-sounding answers rather than looking up verified truth.
- Its quality depends heavily on the data it learned from.

A good analogy is a student who has read a vast library and gotten good at predicting what comes next. They're fluent and useful, but they still need fact-checking.
